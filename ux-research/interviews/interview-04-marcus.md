# Interview 04 — Marcus W.
*Simulated participant | Conducted: 2026-05-03*

**Profile:**
- Divorced, single dad (has kids every other weekend)
- 2 kids: Jordan (9), Aiden (12)
- Location: Crown Heights, Brooklyn, NY
- Occupation: Marketing manager at a media company
- HHI: ~$88K (sole income; pays child support)
- Planning role: Sole decision maker for his weekends with kids
- Tech comfort: High — digital native, uses apps extensively, active on social media

---

## Section 1 — Context & Habits

**Tell me about your family.**

So I have two kids — Jordan, who's nine, and Aiden, who just turned twelve. I have them every other weekend, Friday night through Sunday. That's my window. And I feel it. Every time I pick them up I'm aware of the clock in a way that I don't think parents with full-time custody feel as acutely. These two days need to count. Not in a stressful way, I don't want to overload them — but in a "let's do something real together" way.

Jordan is curious about everything — animals, nature, building things. Aiden is entering that phase where cool matters. He's very into gaming, sneakers, anything that feels grown-up. He'll come with me but he's got his phone out the whole time unless something genuinely gets his attention. The gap between them is only three years but it feels wider than that right now.

**How do your weekends typically look?**

I pick them up Friday, we get food together — that's already quality time, they're chatty over dinner. Saturday is the main event. I try to do a real activity — something we can talk about. Sunday is usually lighter, maybe something in the neighborhood or we cook together. But Saturday I put real effort into.

**When do you start planning?**

The week before they're coming. I have a reminder in my calendar Thursday of the prior week. I don't want to be scrambling Friday — I want to pick them up and already know what we're doing tomorrow.

**How do you find activities?**

Instagram is actually pretty useful — I follow some Brooklyn and NYC family accounts. Google. Time Out New York Kids. I've asked ChatGPT a couple times and gotten decent starting points. My main issue isn't finding things to do in NYC — there's infinite stuff. My issue is finding things Aiden will actually engage with while also being appropriate for a nine-year-old. Those Venn diagrams barely overlap.

**How often does your plan actually happen?**

The logistics always work — I'm pretty good at planning. The emotional outcome is more variable. Aiden goes somewhere between 40-60% "actually engaged" depending on what we do. Jordan is almost always into it. The imbalance bothers me.

---

## Section 2 — Frustrations & Goals

**What's the hardest part of planning?**

The age gap is the core challenge. In NYC there's a huge amount for young kids and a huge amount for adults. The twelve-year-old tween zone is a wasteland. I want Aiden to feel like I picked this for him specifically, not that he's getting dragged along to his little sister's activity. But if I skew too old for Aiden, Jordan is left out. I basically have to find things that accidentally work for both, and those are rare.

**A plan that fell apart?**

A few months ago I found this escape room — I thought Aiden would love the puzzle-solving, and Jordan is sharp, she'd be fine. The age requirement was 12+. We showed up and they wouldn't let Jordan in because she was nine. We were on the sidewalk outside and Jordan started crying, and Aiden — credit to him — said he didn't want to do it if Jordan couldn't come. We ended up just going to the Brooklyn Museum across the street. Which was actually fine and we had a good time, but I felt terrible. I should have checked the age policy.

**What do you wish you could stop thinking about?**

Whether Aiden is going to be on his phone the whole time. That's the thing that haunts me when I'm planning. The activity could be objectively great, but if Aiden decides it's not "his thing" before we even walk in, that energy affects the whole day. And I can't force presence. So I'm essentially trying to find activities that will bypass his adolescent "this is for kids" filter without sacrificing Jordan.

**Unexpected cost or logistics surprises?**

Yeah, the escape room age thing was a logistics surprise. Also — parking in Brooklyn is just not a variable. I don't drive. So I'm always planning around subway access. No subway, no activity. But apps don't know that.

**Weather?**

It complicates things. My backup is usually something in a museum. But I've used up the obvious ones — we've done the Natural History Museum more times than I can count. I want new indoor options, not just the safe defaults.

**What are you really trying to accomplish?**

I want the weekend to feel like we have our own world. That's the best way I can put it. These are my two days. I want them to have memories of weekends with dad that are specific — not "we watched TV" but "remember that weird underground art thing in Dumbo" or "remember when we went to that robot-making workshop." I'm building a bank of memories in forty-eight hours every two weeks.

**Budget?**

It's real. I'm paying child support, single income. I can do one meaningful paid activity per weekend — $50-80 total. I can't do two expensive things. So I'll do one main paid thing and one free thing.

**Age and stage as a filter?**

Constantly. I have a mental age bracket for Aiden — it can't feel "little kid" but it can't require adult supervision to be enjoyable either. That bracket is narrower than I'd like.

## Section 3 — Concept Reaction

**[Concept read aloud]**

The first thing I notice is "kids' ages" — plural. That's good. The second thing I notice is "interests." That's where it'll either succeed or fail for me. My kids' interests are almost incompatible on the surface — Jordan wants animals and nature, Aiden wants things that feel cool and grown-up. If the app just averages those out I'll get something neither of them wants.

But the structure — two-day plan, backup for weather, cost included, drive time — that's exactly the format I've been building manually in my head. Getting that in thirty seconds instead of a week of research would be significant.

**What would make you trust it?**

If it shows it understands Aiden's age specifically. Not "family-friendly" — that's a red flag. I want to see something that acknowledges "a twelve-year-old" is different from "a seven-year-old." Specific language. Also if the recommendations are New York-specific and not "visit a local park" generic.

**What would you verify?**

Age policy. After the escape room situation, that's now the first thing I check on any venue. Every time. Also subway accessibility — I need to know how to get there without a car.

**What's missing?**

Transit directions, not just Google Maps links. For NYC the app should know "subway or rideshare" is the transportation mode, not driving. The parking info is meaningless to me. Also a way to note that I want activities that feel grown-up enough for Aiden without leaving Jordan behind. That's a specific constraint the app needs to understand.

**When does it work vs. not work?**

Works: the Thursday before I pick up the kids, when I need to finalize a plan. Works especially on off-weekends when I'm planning ahead so I'm not scrambling. Doesn't work: when Aiden has something he specifically wants to do (like a game release or a friend's thing), or when Jordan has requested something specific. Maybe 20% of weekends there's already a kid-driven agenda.

**[UI walkthrough]**

I'd put Jordan as 9 and Aiden as 12. ...The interests field — I'd type something like "one kid loves animals/nature, one is into tech and gaming/street culture." I don't know how the app interprets that but let me see what it gives me. ...The cost breakdown is directly useful — I have an $80 ceiling. ...I want to see transportation mode — I'd select "no car / transit." ...The indoor backup is useful but in NYC "indoor" means hundreds of options — I need it to give me something specific, not just "try an indoor activity."

**What's confusing?**

I don't know if I should put in one interest list for the family or separate ones per kid. And the app assumes a car — the "drive time" framing doesn't work for me.

**What would you change?**

Transportation mode selector. And a field that says "tween-friendly" or some way to flag that I have a pre-teen who needs to not feel like the activity is for little kids.

**Anything you'd never use?**

If it kept giving me "family-friendly" activities that are clearly targeted at 5-8 year olds, I'd stop using it.

## Section 4 — Feature Depth

**One primary + one backup — enough?**

Yes. But I'd love a "quick one" option — a free or very low cost activity nearby for Sunday when I want something spontaneous and easy. Saturday gets the main plan; Sunday gets a loose suggestion.

**Cost breakdown?**

Very important. The $80 ceiling is real. I need to know entry plus whatever else before I commit.

**Drive time?**

Not relevant to me — I need transit time instead. If it gave me "30 min by subway from Crown Heights" that would be genuinely useful.

**Profile memory?**

Yes, but specifically I'd want it to track what Aiden reacted well to vs. poorly, not just what we've been to. Over time I want it to learn that Aiden engages with making/building things and street culture, not passive museum experiences.

**Sharing?**

I'd share with their mom sometimes so she knows what we did — helps with co-parenting communication. And I might show Aiden the plan ahead of time to get him bought in, which sometimes works with him.

**Blocklists?**

Immediately: anything with an age minimum that would exclude Jordan. And anything Aiden would describe as "for babies" — which includes most bounce houses, character-themed things, and anything with the word "junior" in it.

**One thing that would make it a must-have?**

The ability to put in two kids with diverging interests and get an activity that actually bridges them — not averages them. Something that Jordan can engage with at a nine-year-old level AND Aiden can engage with at a twelve-year-old level. Finding those things manually takes me hours. If the app could surface them, it would be genuinely valuable.

## Section 5 — Success Metrics & WTP

**How would you know it was working?**

If Aiden voluntarily talks about the activity afterward. That's the metric. He's twelve — if he's telling me about it, or mentioning it to his mom, that's a hit. Jordan is easy to please; Aiden is the hard case.

**"This saved my weekend"?**

A Saturday where both kids were present and engaged — phones put away, actual conversations, genuine shared experience. If the app surfaces something that achieves that, it has done its job.

**If it disappeared?**

I'd feel it. Every-other-weekend custody means I can't afford many "meh" Saturdays. I've learned how to plan but it takes real time. Going back to that would sting.

**Would you pay?**

Yes. $8-10 a month. My constraint is that I'm managing two budgets in a sense — household plus child support. But $10 is worth it if it consistently surfaces good Aiden-friendly options.

**Booking?**

Neutral. I book things myself fine. What I'd pay a small premium for is the transit routing — if it integrated with the subway and gave me door-to-door transit directions, that would be legitimately useful in NYC.

**Who else?**

Every divorced or separated parent who has kids on a schedule. The every-other-weekend dynamic creates a very specific pressure around quality time that full-time parents don't feel the same way. There are a lot of us.

---

## Section 6 — Wrap-Up

**Anything we haven't talked about?**

The custody schedule dimension. I think a lot of apps design for nuclear families where both parents are there every weekend. For me, the stakes are different. If I have a bad weekend, I can't "make it up" the next weekend — those two days are gone. That pressure changes how I'd use this app. I'm not browsing for ideas. I'm relying on it.

**One thing for the team?**

Build for the divorced parent. We have constrained time, we're often single-income, and the quality bar for those two days is higher than you'd think. We're also a growing demographic. If the app understood "every other weekend" as a use case it would immediately differentiate from every other family activity tool.
